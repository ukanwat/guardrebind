\section{Guards and Threat Model}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Guards.}
Shieldstral-1.0-3B~\citep{calvi2026shieldstral} takes a fixed system prompt and a user message with three plain-text fields, \texttt{<Instruct>}, \texttt{<Query>} and \texttt{<Document>} (\cref{fig:anatomy}). % src: src/prompt.py (verbatim from the model card)
The developer writes the first two fields, which together form the deployment's \emph{policy}, and interpolates the content into the third.
The Shieldstral paper describes no special-token delimiters and reports no prompt-injection evaluation.
We read its score as $P(\text{yes})$, the probability of ``yes'' renormalised over the two answers.
Llama Guard~\citep{inan2023llama,grattafiori2024llama,chi2024llama,meta2025llamaguard4} wraps the conversation in plain-text \texttt{<BEGIN CONVERSATION>} and \texttt{<END CONVERSATION>} lines, and its prompt holds a fixed category list but no developer-written policy for content to override.
gpt-oss-safeguard-20B~\citep{openai2025gptosssafeguard} was post-trained to follow an instruction hierarchy~\citep{wallace2024instruction}.
Its policy sits in the system role, behind a role boundary the content cannot cross: the design one would choose as a mitigation.
Our policy asks it to answer 1 for a violation and 0 otherwise. % src: src/crossmodel.py OSS_POLICY; src/bench/api_bench.py

\paragraph{Deployment.}
A developer places a guard $G$ in front of a downstream system and chooses its policy and input format.
Untrusted content $c$ goes into the \emph{content slot} (Shieldstral's \texttt{<Document>} field).
It may be a user message, a retrieved passage, a tool result in an agent loop or an earlier turn of a conversation.
$G$ \emph{clears} $c$, judging it safe, if its score is below a threshold $\tau$ ($\tau = 0.5$ unless stated).
A \emph{label-only} guard returns a verdict and no score, and clears $c$ if the verdict is safe.

\paragraph{Attacker.}
\label{sec:threat}
The attacker controls only $c$.
They do not control the instruction, the query, the system prompt, decoding or the threshold.
Against the attack and the benchmark (\cref{sec:attack,sec:benchmark}) they are black-box: they see no scores, and we test attackers who know the deployment's query exactly and attackers who do not.
Against the defense (\cref{sec:evaluation}) they know the defense.
They have the defended weights, the boundary mechanism and the training recipe, may query the defended guard's score and may use gradients.

\paragraph{Goal and scope.}
The attacker wants a harmful \emph{request} $h$ to be cleared while it stays present and readable in the forwarded content.
A request is a harmful text from a public benchmark that $G$ flags on its own.
Attacks that remove, paraphrase or obfuscate $h$ are out of scope, which separates target rebinding from evasion that hides harmful content from a classifier~\citep{hackett2025bypassing,mangaokar2024prp}.
We also exclude attackers who control the guard's prompt or weights, multimodal content, and denial of service.

\paragraph{Success criterion.}
Only \emph{flagged} requests count, meaning those the deployment flags with no attack.
The flag rule depends on the setting: $G(h) > 0.9$ in the Shieldstral confirmatory study, $G(h) \ge 0.5$ in \guardrebind{} and the defense evaluation, and a majority of three sampled \texttt{unsafe} verdicts for label-only guards. % src: PROTOCOL.md (v1), PROTOCOL_v2.md (Metrics)
The \emph{attack payload} $p$ is the text the attacker adds to $h$.
The attack succeeds if $G$ clears $h \oplus p$, where $\oplus$ is concatenation.
The \emph{bypass rate} (or \emph{bypass}) is the fraction of flagged requests on which it succeeds, reported with a Wilson 95\% interval.
We measure success at the guard, not at a downstream model.
