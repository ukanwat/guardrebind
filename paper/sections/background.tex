\section{Guards and Threat Model}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{How guards receive content}

\paragraph{Shieldstral.}
Shieldstral-1.0-3B~\citep{calvi2026shieldstral} answers a yes/no question about a document.
Its model card specifies a fixed system prompt and a user message with three plain-text fields, \texttt{<Instruct>}, \texttt{<Query>} and \texttt{<Document>} (\cref{fig:anatomy}); the developer writes the first two and interpolates the content into the third. % src: src/prompt.py (verbatim from the model card)
The paper describes no special-token delimiters and reports no prompt-injection evaluation.
We read the score as $P(\text{yes})$ from the yes/no logits renormalised over the two answers.

\paragraph{Llama Guard.}
Llama Guard~\citep{inan2023llama} and its successors~\citep{grattafiori2024llama,chi2024llama,meta2025llamaguard4} grade a conversation against a fixed category taxonomy (S1--S14 for Llama Guard~4).
The chat template wraps the conversation in plain-text \texttt{<BEGIN CONVERSATION>} and \texttt{<END CONVERSATION>} lines, and the model emits \texttt{safe} or \texttt{unsafe} plus a category.
There is no policy in the prompt for the content to override.

\paragraph{gpt-oss-safeguard.}
gpt-oss-safeguard-20B and -120B~\citep{openai2025gptosssafeguard} reason over a developer-written policy placed in the system role and were post-trained to follow an instruction hierarchy~\citep{wallace2024instruction}; the content is a separate user message.
This is the design one would choose as a mitigation: the policy sits behind a role boundary the content cannot cross.

\subsection{Threat model}
\label{sec:threat}

\paragraph{Deployment.}
A developer places a guard $G$ in front of a downstream system, chooses the instruction, grading question (or policy) and format, and inserts untrusted content $c$ into the content slot.
If $G$ clears $c$, $c$ is forwarded unchanged.
Content arrives through one of four channels: (i)~a user message in a chat deployment; (ii)~a retrieved passage in retrieval-augmented generation; (iii)~a tool result in an agent loop, where clearing the guard may cause an action to run; and (iv)~an earlier turn of a multi-turn history.

\paragraph{Attacker.}
The attacker controls only $c$.
They do not control the instruction, the grading question, the system prompt, decoding or the threshold, and they do not see scores.
For the attack and the measurement (\cref{sec:attack,sec:benchmark}) the attacker is black-box, with knowledge of the deployment's grading question ranging from exact to none (\cref{sec:robustness}).
For the defense (\cref{sec:evaluation}) the attacker knows the defense: they have the defended weights, the boundary mechanism and the training recipe, and may optimise content with gradients.

\paragraph{Goal and scope.}
The attacker wants a harmful payload $h$, which $G$ flags on its own, to be cleared while remaining present and readable in the forwarded content.
Attacks that remove, paraphrase or obfuscate $h$ are out of scope; this separates target rebinding from evasion that hides harmful content from a classifier~\citep{hackett2025bypassing,mangaokar2024prp}.
Also out of scope: attackers who control the guard's prompt or weights, multimodal content, and denial of service by making benign content flagged (we measure the latter as over-refusal, not as an attack goal).

\paragraph{Success criterion.}
Only \emph{screened} payloads count: those the honest deployment flags with no attack.
The screen is $G(h) > 0.9$ for the Shieldstral confirmatory study, a majority \texttt{unsafe} verdict over three samples for the label-only guards, and $G(h) \ge 0.5$ in \guardrebind{} and the defense evaluation. % src: PROTOCOL.md (v1), PROTOCOL_v2.md (Metrics)
An attack succeeds on $h$ if $G(h \oplus p) < \tau$, where $p$ is the attacker's payload and $\tau = 0.5$ unless stated.
The \emph{bypass rate} is the fraction of screened payloads on which the attack succeeds, reported with a Wilson 95\% interval.
