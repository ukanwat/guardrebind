\section{Limitations and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The confirmatory study covers one guard, one English corpus and short text-only documents, and the cross-model checks are label-only on 30 requests (\cref{sec:core,sec:crossmodel}). Every bypass rate counts only items the guard flags without an attack, and Shieldstral misses about half of harmful items to begin with (clean recall $47\%$ on the test split). % src: r2_test_base.json recall 0.471
Bypass is an upper bound on end-to-end harm, since we did not measure whether a downstream model acts on the request, and we did not test retrieval, tool-result or multi-turn channels.
Several results rest on small or single runs: the Qwen3Guard task probe (40 items), the adaptive attacks (50 items, 20 for GCG, none on B$+$T), and every trained condition except T, which has two seeds. The smaller guards and naive adversarial training were tested only with fixed decoys.
On the defense side, our held-out results cover appended forms only, and the defense does nothing against searched benign prompts (\cref{sec:adaptive}). Segment-max can miss harm that exists only in combination, and we ran no composition attack. We do not claim general prompt-injection robustness~\citep{pant2026inseparability}.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
A guard that reads its task and the content as one plain-text stream grades the last complete task it sees, so forged content gets a harmful request approved unchanged. Segment-max training closes this channel for held-out appended attacks and keeps the base guard's judgement, but an adaptive attacker with score access still wins, partly without rebinding. Deployers should train the guard, treat a content boundary as a cheap extra, and never let an agent act on a guard verdict alone. Defenses against rebinding should be tested against prepended structure and reported next to a plain-text control.
