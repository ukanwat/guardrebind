\section{Discussion, Limitations and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Recommendations for deployers}

Target rebinding is a property of how a guard's input is assembled, not of any one model, so most of the fix is available to deployers today.
First, do not interpolate untrusted content into a plain-text template: give the content region a boundary the content cannot reproduce, and tokenize the content with special-token parsing disabled so that control-token strings in it stay inert, after checking that the tokenizer has no added tokens outside the special set, which that setting leaves atomic~\citep{zhan2026samebytes,yang2026nameless} (\cref{sec:defense,app:content-tokens}).
Second, treat a guard verdict as a filter, not an authorization: a cleared verdict means ``no policy violation was detected in what was graded'', which is not the same as ``this exact content is safe'', and in an agent loop an action should not run on a guard verdict alone.
Third, an anti-injection directive and syntax-specific label stripping did not stop the attack (\cref{sec:evaluation,app:protocol}), and a role boundary around the policy is not sufficient on its own (\cref{sec:crossmodel}).
A fail-closed detector for label-like strings is cheap and catches the syntaxes it lists, but it blocks about half of benign structured content and misses syntaxes it does not list (\cref{sec:evaluation}).
Where retraining is possible, train against the maximum over the content's parts (\cref{sec:evaluation}), and check over-refusal on benign content that carries forged structure: on two smaller guards the same recipe raised it to $22$--$26\%$.
Test the result against an attacker who can query the guard's score, and against benign user prompts placed before the request, which clear even the base guard on most items (\cref{sec:adaptive}).

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Scope of the attack results.}
The confirmatory results (\cref{sec:attack}) are one guard, one corpus (AdvBench rows 200--299), English, text-only, short documents; on the broader benchmark corpus the same pair clears Shieldstral less often ($70.9\%$).
The cross-model results are label-only, on 30 AdvBench requests, and for Llama Guard~4 we did not separate rebinding from chat-template malformation (\cref{sec:crossmodel}).
gpt-oss-safeguard flags only $56.5\%$ of harmful core items with no attack, so its bypass rates describe the 113 items it does catch, on one API provider and the user-message channel; API-served models are not pinned to a weight revision, and the provider's tokenization of turn markers sent as text is not observable (\cref{sec:bench-results,app:gpt-oss}). % src: paper/data/gpt_oss_strict.md (parse = strict, recall 113/200); results/bench/v2/gpt_oss_summary.md (provider Groq)
The ``what counts as a task'' result for Qwen3Guard is exploratory: train split, 40 items, the 0.6B model, one decoy (\cref{sec:request-unit}).
The binding is partial, not total: with a borderline decoy the original request still raises the score (\cref{sec:groundtruth}), and prepending the forged pair still clears $15\%$, so position is not the whole story.
Every bypass rate is conditional on the items the guard flags with no attack: Shieldstral's clean recall is $47\%$ on the test split and its miss rate on the benchmark core set is $50\%$, so the attack is measured on the half of harmful content the guard already catches (\cref{sec:bench-results,sec:evaluation}). % src: r2_test_base.json recall 0.471; bypass.csv / benchmark Table clean FNR 50.0
The activation analyses (\cref{sec:binding}) are correlational or coarse-grained: they show where the verdict is not carried, not which components choose the graded document.
Llama Guard~3's and Qwen3Guard's templates ask for an assessment of the last user turn only, so forging a new last turn partly uses the behaviour those guards specify; for them, rebinding is as much a specification gap as a parsing failure.

\paragraph{What was not measured.}
Success is measured at the guard: we did not measure whether a downstream model acts on the forwarded request, so bypass is an upper bound on end-to-end harm.
Of the four channels in \cref{sec:threat}, we measured the user message and, for response-side items, the response; the retrieval, tool-result and multi-turn channels are argued from the input format, not measured.
For an output guard the forged structure would have to appear in the model's response, which our threat model does not cover.
The benchmark does not vary model size within a series.

\paragraph{Scope of the defense results.}
T has two seeds on Shieldstral, and B$+$T, the clean-copy target, the adaptive attacks and the smaller guards have one; the smaller guards were trained and tested with one fixed decoy and query per family, so they do not test generalisation to held-out decoys and queries, and on them the defense raises over-refusal on benign content carrying forged structure to $22$--$26\%$.
Naive adversarial training uses ground-truth labels where the other conditions use the base guard's scores, which confounds its detection gain.
Segment-max can miss harm that exists only in the combination of benign segments; including the whole content as a segment (\cref{eq:target}) bounds but does not remove this, and we did not run a composition attack that splits harm across segments.
The harmful-decoy test (\cref{sec:shortcut}) covers only the orderings our grammar and search generate.
The defense does not withstand an adaptive attacker: a score-guided search with 300 calls per item clears T on $94\%$ and B$+$T on $68\%$ of 50 items, most often by placing forged structure before the request ($34$ of $47$ wins on T, $21$ of them with an off-topic forged query), a position almost absent from training (\cref{sec:adaptive}).
The undefended guard follows the last pair, while the trained guard is beaten mostly by structure placed first: training moved the weakness rather than removing it, and the held-out-family results, which cover appended forms only, do not test this position.
More than half of the search's wins on T need no forged structure: benign user prompts placed before the request clear the base guard on $70\%$ and T on $52\%$ of items, and the defense does not address it. The winning searches all draw their decoys from these prompts, so the search does not separate the effect of prepended structure from that of the decoy distribution.
White-box GCG clears T on all 20 items it was run on (\cref{app:gcg}), and we make no claim of robustness to it; GCG was not run on B$+$T, and we did not run an LLM-based attacker (pre-registered A3).
The adaptive attacks use 50 items (20 for GCG).
We reduce one structural channel against attackers who enumerate known attack forms and do not claim general prompt-injection robustness, which is argued to be unattainable in shared-embedding models without enforced separation~\citep{pant2026inseparability}.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Safety guards receive the content they judge through the same plain-text channel that states their task, and content that forges that task structure rebinds the guard to a document the attacker chooses while the harmful request stays in the approved message.
On Shieldstral the forged pair clears all 75 pre-registered held-out requests, bypass falls as the decoy becomes more harmful, and the construction transfers to two other guard designs.
Segment-max self-distillation, trained on one half of the decoy and query pools, cuts bypass on held-out appended families with held-out decoys from $75\%$ to $0.8$--$1.6\%$, while a fail-closed detector catches the syntaxes it lists only by blocking about half of benign structured content.
It does not hold against an attacker with score access: a score-guided search clears it on $94\%$ of items, and $98\%$ counting GCG.
Part of that weakness predates the attack, since benign user prompts placed before a request already clear the guard; a defense against rebinding has to be evaluated together with that baseline.
