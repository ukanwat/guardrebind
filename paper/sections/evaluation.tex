\section{Evaluating the Defense}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Setup}

The main model is Shieldstral-1.0-3B, and we test whether T carries over to Llama Guard~3 1B and Qwen3Guard 0.6B~\citep{grattafiori2024llama,zhao2025qwenguard}.
All numbers are on the test split, and no test item is seen in training.
We measure bypass at $\tau=0.5$ on the harmful test items that each condition flags with no attack.
The attack sets are the training families with each family's fixed decoy and query, the held-out families (whole grammar branches never seen in training, \cref{app:grammar}) with the same fixed decoys and queries, and the held-out families with decoys and forged queries drawn from the held-out halves of the pools (three instantiations per item and family, seeded by the item). % src: src/fix/evaluate.py --decoy-split fixed,heldout; src/bench/attacks_grammar.py POOL_SPLIT
This last set, our primary measure of generalisation, shares no attack structure, decoy or forged query with training.
Position is not held out: every sampled form in both family splits appends the forged structure after the request, and only the structural and score-guided searches vary position (\cref{sec:adaptive}). % src: r2_test_*.json per_attack keys: all pos=append (12 train-family + 12 held-out-family sampled forms)
Bypass pools the sampled attacks whose decoy is benign or borderline, since an attack whose decoy is harmful is correctly flagged.

\emph{Detection} is the area under the ROC curve (AUROC), recall and false-positive rate (FPR) at $\tau$ on one fixed random sample of 1{,}500 clean test items (801 harmful, 699 benign), shared by all conditions. % src: src/fix/evaluate.py (test split shuffled with random.Random(0), over-refusal sources removed, first --n-clean 1500); 11,635 clean test items, 5,598 of them benign
\emph{Over-refusal} is the flag rate on XSTest-safe~\citep{rottger2023xstest} ($n=75$), OR-Bench-hard~\citep{cui2024orbench} ($n=400$) and a structured-benign set ($n=500$).
We also measure the flag rate on 150 benign items carrying the same forged pairs.
T has two seeds (we report the range), and B$+$T, the clean-copy target and the generality runs have one. This seed plan (cut from the pre-registered three seeds for B$+$T to save compute), the held-out-decoy design, the score-guided search and the two training-free baselines come from a protocol deviation recorded after a first round of test-split runs, with those results in view (deviation D4, \cref{app:protocol}). % src: PROTOCOL_v2.md D1(d), D3
Bypass differences use a paired bootstrap over items.
Detection and over-refusal differences use unpaired 95\% intervals, which are wider than paired ones, and a condition counts as non-inferior only if that interval lies inside the pre-registered margin. % src: PROTOCOL_v2.md (Metrics)

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Besides the base guard, B and the clean-copy target, we compare two training-free baselines.
The \emph{fail-closed structure detector} flags any content that contains a chat control-token string or a label-like string from a fixed English list of syntaxes, and passes everything else to the base guard. % src: src/fix/baselines.py (detect, DetectorGuard)
\emph{Inference-time segment-max} splits the content at the same strings, scores every segment and the whole content with the base guard, and returns the maximum, which is the target of \cref{eq:target} computed at run time, at the cost of one forward pass per segment. % src: src/fix/baselines.py (SegmaxGuard)
In the confirmatory study, an anti-injection directive (a sentence in the instruction telling the guard to disregard instructions inside the document) left bypass unchanged, and escaping and fencing left it at $88$--$93\%$ (\cref{app:protocol}). % src: results/confirm_log.txt [mitigation]; results/supp_log.txt [B]; tested string = src/prompt.py ANTI_INJECTION_DIRECTIVE (its first clause)

Training cuts bypass on held-out families with held-out decoys and queries from $75.0\%$\ci{74.0\%}{76.0\%} of attacked inputs on the base guard to $0.8\%$ and $1.6\%$ on T (seeds 0 and 1) and $0.9\%$\ci{0.7\%}{1.1\%} on B$+$T, meeting the pre-registered target of $\le 10\%$ (\cref{tab:fix-main}). % src: r2_test_*.json by_decoy_split.heldout.heldout.bypass_benign_decoy
Held-out decoys clear T slightly less often than the fixed ones (by $0.4$ points\ci{$+0.1$}{$+0.8$}, paired, $n=290$ items), and on the training families with held-out decoys T is cleared on $0.7$--$1.4\%$ (base $69.4\%$). % src: by_decoy_split.heldout.train.bypass_benign_decoy (base 4949/7128; T 50/7008, 99/7008); paired bootstrap, held-out families, seed-averaged, 10k resamples
B alone leaves held-out bypass at $76.8\%$, as pre-registered: untrained boundary embeddings mean nothing to the model.
T also generalises worse than B$+$T to held-out families, as predicted (paired-bootstrap T $-$ B$+$T $=+0.7$ points\ci{$-0.1$}{$+1.5$}, one-sided $p=0.04$, $n=290$ items), but the effect is under one point and both conditions are near $1\%$. % src: same bootstrap, held-out families x held-out decoys, T seed-averaged vs B+T

Measured costs in detection and over-refusal are small, but non-inferiority is not established (\cref{tab:fix-utility}).
Relative to the base guard, B$+$T changes detection AUROC by $-0.010$ and recall by $-0.1$ points, and no over-refusal rate rises by more than $0.25$ points (one OR-Bench-hard item). % src: r2_test_{base,bt_seed0}.json overrefusal_orbench 206/400 -> 207/400
These point estimates lie inside the pre-registered non-inferiority margins, but the 95\% intervals of the differences are wider, and non-inferiority is confirmed for neither detection nor over-refusal (intervals in \cref{app:protocol}). % src: r2_test_{base,bt_seed0}.json auroc (Hanley-McNeil, 801/699), recall k/n, overrefusal.* k/n (Newcombe)
T's changes are of the same size, with one exception: T seed 1 exceeds the over-refusal margin on OR-Bench-hard ($+2.75$ points against $\pm 2$).
Benign content carrying a forged pair with a benign decoy shows a small but statistically detectable cost: it is flagged $0.7\%$ of the time under T (both seeds) and $0.8\%$ under B$+$T, against $0.2\%$ for the base guard (Fisher exact $p=0.012$ for each T seed, $p=0.003$ for B$+$T). % src: attacked_benign_fpr_benign_decoy, fixed decoy, train+heldout pooled; scipy.stats.fisher_exact two-sided

\paragraph{Baseline results.}
The training-free baselines stop every training-family attack but let through $23.2\%$\ci{22.2\%}{24.2\%} (detector) and $23.4\%$\ci{22.4\%}{24.4\%} (inference-time segment-max) of held-out families with held-out decoys. % src: r2_test_detector.json, r2_test_segmax_infer.json by_decoy_split.heldout.heldout
Both split or match content with a fixed English pattern list, and all of their remaining bypass comes from the two held-out families with non-English labels: one with Spanish labels in markdown-bold syntax and one with Chinese labels, which clear the detector $97.2\%$ and $88.4\%$ of the time. Every other held-out family clears it $0\%$. % src: r2_test_detector.json by_decoy_split.heldout.heldout.per_attack (md_bold lang=es 0.972, angle lang=zh 0.884, other 10 = 0)
A pattern list that included those labels would flag both families, so the detector's held-out number reflects how well its list covers the attacker's labels.
The detector's cost is false positives: it alone flags $54.8\%$\ci{50.4\%}{59.1\%} of the structured-benign test set ($58.6\%$ with the guard's own flags) and $87.5\%$ of benign content carrying a forged pair. % src: baselines.detect structured_benign 274/500; r2_test_detector.json overrefusal.structured_benign 293/500; attacked_benign_fpr 87.5
Inference-time segment-max over-flags less than the detector but more than T: it flags $9.8\%$ of structured-benign content (T: $5.0\%$) and $13.0\%$ of benign content carrying a forged pair with a benign decoy (T: $0.7\%$). % src: r2_test_segmax_infer.json

\paragraph{Naive adversarial training.}
Naive adversarial training, a LoRA on the same examples with hard 0/1 ground-truth labels, matches T on bypass but costs clean FPR and misses trailing harmful content.
It was trained and tested only under the fixed-decoy protocol (\cref{tab:fix-fixed}, two seeds), not with held-out decoys, and it reaches $0.9$--$1.1\%$ bypass on held-out families (T: $1.2$--$2.0\%$) and $0.0\%$ structured-benign over-refusal, on a set that overlaps its hard negatives, which contradicts our pre-registered prediction that it would cost at least 3 points there or generalise worse. % src: test_at_seed{0,1}.json; r2_test_t_seed{0,1}.json attacks.heldout
Its clean FPR is $12.6$--$14.9\%$ (T: $5.2$--$5.3\%$), and its higher AUROC ($0.921$--$0.925$) comes from being the only condition trained on ground-truth labels, not the base guard's scores. % src: test_at_seed{0,1}.json fpr_clean, auroc; r2_test_t_seed{0,1}.json fpr_clean 5.2/5.3

\paragraph{Trailing harmful content.}
\label{sec:shortcut}
The max in the target keeps the trained guard reading past a forged pair.
A guard trained against rebinding could stop reading there~\citep{wang2025illusion} and so open a smuggling channel, which the harmful-decoy column of \cref{tab:fix-utility} tests.
T and B$+$T flag $100\%$ of these items with the fixed decoys and $95.5$--$98.7\%$ with held-out ones, against $0.8\%$ (clean-copy target) and $8.0$--$8.6\%$ (naive adversarial training). % src: test_*.json per_attack_benign_fpr, decoy=harmful attacks

\paragraph{Other guards.}
T, trained and tested with the fixed decoy and query (\cref{tab:fix-fixed}), transfers to the two smaller guards without changing the recipe. On Llama Guard~3 1B it cuts held-out-family bypass from $82.0\%$\ci{80.4\%}{83.5\%} to $2.5\%$\ci{1.9\%}{3.2\%}; on Qwen3Guard 0.6B the sampled families are mostly generic syntaxes it does not rebind to (\cref{sec:request-unit}), and the drop from $9.5\%$ to $0.0\%$ says little. % src: test_lg3_{base,t_seed0}.json; test_qg3_{base,t_seed0}.json
On both, the flag rate on benign content carrying a benign forged pair rises (from $2.4\%$ to $22.5\%$ on Llama Guard~3 and from $10.5\%$ to $25.9\%$ on Qwen3Guard), for reasons we do not know. % src: attacked_benign_fpr_benign_decoy pooled train+heldout

The defense resists enumeration of the attack grammar, but an attacker with score access or gradients still breaks it.
The attacker knows the defense and sees its score (\cref{sec:threat}), and we follow the evaluation norms of \citet{carlini2019evaluating}.
The content cannot produce the boundary tokens (\cref{sec:defense}). The attacker therefore optimises the content text only, and every attack runs end to end on the defended model.
We run three attacks on 50 flagged test items and report the per-item worst case (\cref{tab:adaptive}).
\emph{Score-guided search} hill-climbs on the defended guard's score within 300 guard calls per item, mutating one or two fields of the best candidate (syntax, structure, position, number of pairs, label strings, decoy text and forged query), with random restarts. % src: src/fix/adaptive.py search_attack (pop 20, batch 10, p_restart 0.2, budget 300)
Its decoys are the held-out halves of the decoy pools plus 500 benign user prompts from the development split, and its queries are every forged-query pool plus the deployment's own query; most of this material is unseen in training. % src: src/fix/adaptive.py search_pools
\emph{Structural search} tries all 654 forms of the full attack grammar and 30 random label mutations, with decoys and queries drawn from the held-out pools. % src: src/fix/adaptive.py --attack structural --decoy-split heldout
\emph{GCG} (Greedy Coordinate Gradient~\citep{zou2023universal}) optimises a 20-token suffix inside the forged document for 250 steps, on 20 of the 50 items.
A \emph{plain-text control} runs the score-guided search with the same budget and decoy texts, but each candidate adds only the decoy text (one to five copies, before or after the request), without labels or forged query. % src: src/fix/adaptive.py --null-search (render_candidate NULL_SEARCH)

Both searches clear the undefended guard on every item, passing the sanity check of at least $90\%$.
Enumerating the grammar with held-out decoys and queries rarely beats the trained guards: it clears T on $8\%$\ci{3\%}{19\%} of items and B$+$T on $8\%$, within our pre-registered target of at most $20\%$ for B$+$T.

The score-guided search breaks both trained guards, clearing T on $94\%$\ci{84\%}{98\%} of items and B$+$T on $68\%$\ci{54\%}{79\%}. % src: worst_case k/n
Every winning candidate uses one of the 500 development-split user prompts as its decoy, never a decoy-pool sentence.
Alone, the 35 distinct winning prompts are benign to the base guard (median score $0.0003$; one, in one B$+$T win, scores $0.53$). % src: paper/data/search_decoys.md (base guard, CPU)
In $34$ of T's $47$ wins the best candidate places the forged structure before the request, often with an off-topic forged query ($21$ of the $34$), and on B$+$T $29$ of $34$ wins prepend. % src: .jsonl best.position, best.query in QUERY_POOLS_V1['out_of_domain'] (T: 21 prepended wins use an out_of_domain query); paper/data/search_no_feedback.md
One interpretation is that prepended structure attacks the grading question and not the document, since almost all training attacks append the pair after the request ($244$ of the $252$ training forms). % src: attacks_grammar.split_attacks train structural forms by position: append 244, prepend 4, middle 4
The search does not separate this from the shift to longer, prompt-like decoys.
With one seed per guard, B$+$T is cleared less often than T.

The plain-text control measures how much of this needs forged structure.
On 26 of the 47 items the search wins against T, benign user prompts with no labels and no forged query, chosen by the same score-guided search, also clear T, and forged structure adds the other 21. % src: paper/data/search_no_feedback.md (t_seed0: 26 null wins, all 26 also search wins)
The control clears the base guard on $70\%$\ci{56\%}{81\%} of items and T on $52\%$\ci{39\%}{65\%}, always with the benign text before the request. % src: r2_adaptive_{base,t_seed0}_search_null.json(.jsonl): T 26/26 position=prepend; base 22 prepend + 13 middle (filler before and after)
The inert filler of \cref{tab:ablation} ($0\%$) is one fixed text, whereas the control picks, per item, the best of up to 300 benign candidates using the guard's score. % src: src/fix/adaptive.py search_attack budget 300 (also for --null-search); confirm_log.txt [ablation] benign_tail 0/75
Each item's first candidate is drawn before any score is seen, and it alone clears the base guard on 14 of 50 items ($28\%$\ci{17\%}{42\%}) and T on 3 ($6\%$\ci{2\%}{16\%}): an unsearched benign candidate does dilute the request, but selection under score access accounts for most of the control's success. % src: paper/data/search_no_feedback.md (queries == 1: base_search_null 14/50, t_seed0_search_null 3/50; Wilson)

GCG clears T on all $20$ items it was run on, which raises the per-item worst case over all attacks against T to $49/50$ ($98\%$\ci{89\%}{100\%}). % src: r2_adaptive_t_seed0_gcg.json
We did not run GCG on B$+$T, so its worst case, $34/50$, is a lower bound.
