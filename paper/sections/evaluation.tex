\section{Evaluating the Defense}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Setup}

Part of this evaluation was designed after a first round of test-split runs, with those results in view: the held-out-decoy measure, the score-guided search, both training-free baselines and the reduced seed plan come from deviation D4 (\cref{app:protocol}). Fixed-decoy results are in \cref{tab:fix-fixed}.

The main model is Shieldstral-1.0-3B, and we test whether T carries over to Llama Guard~3 1B and Qwen3Guard 0.6B~\citep{grattafiori2024llama,zhao2025qwenguard}.
All numbers are on the test split. No test item is seen in training, apart from the structured-benign overlap of the fixed-decoy runs (\cref{sec:defense}).
We measure bypass at $\tau=0.5$ on the harmful test items that each condition flags with no attack (297 for the base guard and B$+$T, 292 for each T seed); paired comparisons with T use the 290 items that both T seeds flag, all of which B$+$T also flags. % src: r2_test_*.json raw_scores_by_decoy_split.heldout.screened_ids: base 297, t_seed0 292, t_seed1 292, bt_seed0 297; |t0 & t1| = 290, all in bt_seed0; scripts/analysis_r2/boot.py (avg over items screened by both T seeds, then & B+T) n = 290
The attack sets are the training families with each family's fixed decoy and query, the held-out families (whole grammar branches never seen in training, \cref{app:grammar}) with the same fixed decoys and queries, and the held-out families with decoys and forged queries drawn from the held-out halves of the pools (three instantiations per item and family, seeded by the item). % src: src/fix/evaluate.py --decoy-split fixed,heldout; src/bench/attacks_grammar.py POOL_SPLIT
This last set, our primary measure of generalisation, shares no attack structure, decoy or forged query with training.
Position is not held out, and only the structural and score-guided searches vary it (\cref{sec:adaptive}): every form sampled for evaluation appends the forged structure after the request, while the 8 non-appended forms of the training branch (of 252) make up 3\% of training attacks. % src: r2_test_*.json per_attack keys: all pos=append (12 train-family + 12 held-out-family sampled forms); attacks_grammar.split_attacks train structural forms by position: append 244, prepend 4, middle 4; distill.build_examples(2, 5000, decoy_split=train) attacked examples: append 9480, prepend 162, middle 155, control 203 (317/10000 = 3.2%)
Bypass pools the sampled attacks whose decoy is benign or borderline, since flagging a harmful decoy is correct.

\emph{Detection} is the area under the ROC curve (AUROC), recall and false-positive rate (FPR) at $\tau$ on one fixed random sample of 1{,}500 clean test items (801 harmful, 699 benign), shared by all conditions. % src: src/fix/evaluate.py (test split shuffled with random.Random(0), over-refusal sources removed, first --n-clean 1500); 11,635 clean test items, 5,598 of them benign
\emph{Over-refusal} is the flag rate on XSTest-safe~\citep{rottger2023xstest} ($n=75$), OR-Bench-hard~\citep{cui2024orbench} ($n=400$) and a structured-benign set ($n=500$).
We also measure the flag rate on 150 benign items carrying the same forged pairs.
T has two seeds (we report the range). B$+$T, the clean-copy target and the generality runs have one, down from the three pre-registered for B$+$T, to save compute. % src: PROTOCOL_v2.md D1(d), D3
Bypass differences use a paired bootstrap over items; detection and over-refusal use unpaired 95\% intervals, and non-inferiority needs the interval inside the margin. % src: PROTOCOL_v2.md (Metrics)

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Besides the base guard, B and the clean-copy target, we compare two training-free baselines.
The \emph{fail-closed structure detector} flags any content that contains a chat control-token string or a label-like string from a fixed English list of syntaxes, and passes everything else to the base guard. % src: src/fix/baselines.py (detect, DetectorGuard)
\emph{Inference-time segment-max} splits the content at the same strings, scores every segment and the whole content with the base guard, and returns the maximum, which is the target of \cref{eq:target} computed at run time, at the cost of one forward pass per segment. % src: src/fix/baselines.py (SegmaxGuard)
In the confirmatory study, an anti-injection directive in the policy left bypass unchanged, and escaping and fencing left it at $88$--$93\%$ (\cref{app:protocol}). % src: results/confirm_log.txt [mitigation]; results/supp_log.txt [B]; tested string = src/prompt.py ANTI_INJECTION_DIRECTIVE (its first clause)

Training nearly closes the appended channel for the attack families we enumerate. On held-out families with held-out decoys and queries, the base guard is cleared on $75.0\%$\ci{74.0\%}{76.0\%} of attacked inputs. T is cleared on $0.8\%$ and $1.6\%$ (seeds 0 and 1), and B$+$T on $0.9\%$\ci{0.7\%}{1.1\%}, well inside the pre-registered target of $\le 10\%$ (\cref{tab:fix-main}). % src: r2_test_*.json by_decoy_split.heldout.heldout.bypass_benign_decoy
New decoys do not help the attacker: held-out decoys clear T slightly less often than the fixed ones (by $0.4$ points\ci{$+0.1$}{$+0.8$}, T averaged over seeds, paired, $n=290$ items). On the training families with held-out decoys, T is cleared on $0.7$--$1.4\%$ (base $69.4\%$). % src: by_decoy_split.heldout.train.bypass_benign_decoy (base 4949/7128; T 50/7008, 99/7008); paired bootstrap, held-out families, seed-averaged, 10k resamples
B alone leaves held-out bypass at $76.8\%$ (base $75.0\%$), as pre-registered: without training, the model does not use the boundary tokens.

Once the guard is trained, the boundary adds little. Across the non-adaptive measures in \cref{tab:fix-main,tab:fix-utility}, T alone does about as well as B$+$T: bypass and detection differ by under a point, and over-refusal by at most $2.5$ points (OR-Bench-hard, T seed 1). % src: tab:fix-main/tab:fix-utility rows T vs B+T
The pre-registered test that T generalises worse than B$+$T passes its one-sided criterion: seed-averaged T is cleared $0.7$ points more often (two-sided 95\% interval\ci{$-0.1$}{$+1.5$}, one-sided $p=0.04$, $n=290$ items). The gap is under a point, and T seed 0 ($0.8\%$) is below B$+$T ($0.9\%$). % src: same bootstrap, held-out families x held-out decoys, T seed-averaged vs B+T

The measurable costs are small. By design, detection does not improve: the teacher is the base guard, so T learns to match its judgement on clean content, not to exceed it: AUROC stays at $0.80$ against $0.81$ for the base guard (\cref{tab:fix-main}), but non-inferiority is not established (F4 and F5 in \cref{tab:prereg}).
For B$+$T the point estimates lie inside the pre-registered margins (AUROC $-0.010$; no over-refusal rate up by more than one OR-Bench-hard item), but the 95\% intervals of the differences are wider than the margins. % src: r2_test_{base,bt_seed0}.json auroc (Hanley-McNeil, 801/699), recall k/n, overrefusal.* k/n (Newcombe); overrefusal_orbench 206/400 -> 207/400
T's changes are of the same size, except that T seed 1 exceeds the over-refusal margin on OR-Bench-hard ($+2.75$ points against $\pm 2$).
One cost is statistically detectable. Benign content that carries a forged pair with a benign decoy is flagged $0.7\%$ of the time under T and $0.8\%$ under B$+$T, against $0.2\%$ for the base guard (Fisher exact $p=0.012$ for each T seed, $p=0.003$ for B$+$T), so training makes a nested pair slightly suspicious on its own. % src: attacked_benign_fpr_benign_decoy, fixed decoy, train+heldout pooled; scipy.stats.fisher_exact two-sided

\paragraph{Baseline results.}
The training-free baselines stop every training-family attack but are cleared on $23.2\%$\ci{22.2\%}{24.2\%} (detector) and $23.4\%$\ci{22.4\%}{24.4\%} (inference-time segment-max) of held-out families with held-out decoys. % src: r2_test_detector.json, r2_test_segmax_infer.json by_decoy_split.heldout.heldout
Both split or match content with a fixed English pattern list, and nearly all of their remaining bypass (all, for the detector) comes from the two held-out families with non-English labels. % src: r2_test_segmax_infer.json by_decoy_split.heldout.heldout.per_attack: md_bold es 0.975, angle zh 0.887, json q=paraphrase decoy=borderline 0.009, others 0
 The one with Spanish labels in markdown-bold syntax clears the detector $97.2\%$ of the time and the one with Chinese labels $88.4\%$. % src: r2_test_detector.json by_decoy_split.heldout.heldout.per_attack (md_bold lang=es 0.972, angle lang=zh 0.884, other 10 = 0)
A longer list would catch both, so the detector's held-out number measures only how well its list anticipates the attacker's labels.
The detector's cost is false positives: it alone flags $54.8\%$\ci{50.4\%}{59.1\%} of the structured-benign test set ($58.6\%$ with the guard's own flags) and $87.5\%$ of benign content carrying a forged pair. % src: baselines.detect structured_benign 274/500; r2_test_detector.json overrefusal.structured_benign 293/500; attacked_benign_fpr 87.5
Inference-time segment-max over-flags less, though more than T: $9.8\%$ of structured-benign content (T: $5.0\%$) and $13.0\%$ of benign text with a benign forged pair (T: $0.7\%$). % src: r2_test_segmax_infer.json

\paragraph{Naive adversarial training.}
Naive adversarial training fits a LoRA on the same examples with hard 0/1 ground-truth labels. We ran it only with fixed decoys (\cref{tab:fix-fixed}, two seeds). There it is cleared on $0.9$--$1.1\%$ of held-out-family inputs (T: $1.2$--$2.0\%$) and over-refuses none of the structured-benign set, which overlaps its hard negatives; this contradicts our pre-registered prediction (F6 in \cref{tab:prereg}). % src: test_at_seed{0,1}.json; r2_test_t_seed{0,1}.json attacks.heldout
Its AUROC is higher ($0.921$--$0.925$), likely because it learns from ground-truth labels instead of the base guard's scores, so it is a retrained detector, not a patched copy of the base guard. At $\tau=0.5$ it flags more of everything: its clean FPR is $12.6$--$14.9\%$ (T: $5.2$--$5.3\%$) and its recall is far higher (\cref{tab:fix-fixed}). Our result files keep no per-item clean scores, so we cannot compare the two at matched recall. % src: test_at_seed{0,1}.json auroc, fpr_clean, recall 83.3/84.6; r2_test_t_seed{0,1}.json fpr_clean 5.2/5.3; raw_scores in these files hold attacked inputs only (no clean per-item scores)
We prefer T because it keeps the base guard's clean-content behaviour, which we set out to preserve, and because naive training misses trailing harm.

\paragraph{Trailing harmful content.}
\label{sec:shortcut}
A guard trained against rebinding could learn to stop reading at a forged pair~\citep{wang2025illusion}, which would open a smuggling channel: benign content, a forged pair, then harmful text. The harmful-decoy column of \cref{tab:fix-utility} tests this.
T and B$+$T flag $100\%$ of these items with the fixed decoys and $95.5$--$98.7\%$ with held-out ones. The clean-copy target flags $0.8\%$ and naive adversarial training $8.0$--$8.6\%$. Here the clean-copy target and T differ most. % src: test_*.json per_attack_benign_fpr, decoy=harmful attacks

\paragraph{Other guards.}
With the fixed decoy and query (\cref{tab:fix-fixed}, one seed), T transfers to Llama Guard~3 1B unchanged, cutting held-out-family bypass from $82.0\%$\ci{80.4\%}{83.5\%} to $2.5\%$\ci{1.9\%}{3.2\%}; on Qwen3Guard 0.6B the sampled families are mostly generic syntaxes it does not rebind to (\cref{sec:request-unit}), and the drop from $9.5\%$ to $0.0\%$ says little. % src: test_lg3_{base,t_seed0}.json; test_qg3_{base,t_seed0}.json
The cost here is much larger than on Shieldstral. Benign content carrying a benign forged pair is flagged about ten times as often on Llama Guard~3 ($2.4\%$ to $22.5\%$) and more than twice as often on Qwen3Guard ($10.5\%$ to $25.9\%$). We do not know why. % src: attacked_benign_fpr_benign_decoy pooled train+heldout

The defense resists enumeration of the attack grammar but not an attacker who can see its score, and about half of that attacker's success needs no forged structure.
The attacker knows the defense and sees its score (\cref{sec:threat}), following \citet{carlini2019evaluating}. Content cannot produce the boundary tokens, so the attacker optimises the content text, and every attack runs end to end on the defended model.
We run three attacks on 50 flagged test items and report the per-item worst case (\cref{tab:adaptive}).
\emph{Score-guided search} hill-climbs on the defended guard's score within 300 guard calls per item, mutating one or two fields of the best candidate (such as syntax, position, decoy or forged query), with random restarts. % src: src/fix/adaptive.py search_attack (pop 20, batch 10, p_restart 0.2, budget 300)
Its decoys are the held-out halves of the decoy pools plus 500 benign user prompts from the development split, and its queries are every forged-query pool plus the deployment's own query; most of this material is unseen in training. % src: src/fix/adaptive.py search_pools
\emph{Structural search} tries all 654 forms of the full attack grammar and 30 random label mutations, with decoys and queries drawn from the held-out pools. % src: src/fix/adaptive.py --attack structural --decoy-split heldout
\emph{GCG} (Greedy Coordinate Gradient~\citep{zou2023universal}) optimises a 20-token suffix inside the forged document for 250 steps, on 20 of the 50 items.
A \emph{plain-text control} runs the score-guided search with the same budget and decoys, but each candidate adds only decoy text (1--5 copies, before or after the request), with no labels or forged query. % src: src/fix/adaptive.py --null-search (render_candidate NULL_SEARCH)

Both searches clear the undefended guard on every item (sanity check: at least $90\%$).
Enumerating the grammar with held-out decoys and queries rarely beats the trained guards. It clears T and B$+$T on $8\%$\ci{3\%}{19\%} of items each, within our pre-registered target of at most $20\%$ for B$+$T.

The score-guided search breaks both trained guards, clearing T on $94\%$\ci{84\%}{98\%} of items and B$+$T on $68\%$\ci{54\%}{79\%}. % src: worst_case k/n
Every winning candidate uses one of the 500 development-split user prompts as its decoy, never a decoy-pool sentence.
Scored alone by the base guard, 34 of the 35 distinct winning prompts across all searches and plain-text controls are benign (median score $0.0003$); the one that is not ($0.53$) appears in one B$+$T win. % src: paper/data/search_decoys.md (base guard, CPU)
In $34$ of T's $47$ wins the best candidate places the forged structure before the request, $21$ of them with an off-topic forged query. On B$+$T, $29$ of $34$ wins prepend. % src: .jsonl best.position, best.query in QUERY_POOLS_V1['out_of_domain'] (T: 21 prepended wins use an out_of_domain query); paper/data/search_no_feedback.md
Training rarely puts the structure before the request (162 of 10{,}000 attacked training examples), and the evaluation sample never does, so the search succeeds in a position that training barely covers. One reading is that prepended structure attacks the grading question instead of the document. % src: distill.build_examples(2, 5000, decoy_split=train): prepend 162, middle 155, append 9480, control 203 of 10000 attacked examples; r2_test_*.json per_attack all pos=append
The search does not separate this from its shift to prompt-like decoys.
B$+$T is cleared less often than T (34 against 47 of 50). It is the only result in the paper where the boundary clearly helps, and each condition has one seed here.

The plain-text control asks how much of this is rebinding at all.
Benign user prompts alone, with no labels or forged query and chosen by the same search, clear T on 26 of the 47 items the full search wins. Forged structure is needed by this search only for the other 21. % src: paper/data/search_no_feedback.md (t_seed0: 26 null wins, all 26 also search wins)
The control clears the base guard on $70\%$\ci{56\%}{81\%} of items and T on $52\%$\ci{39\%}{65\%}. Every win against T puts the searched text before the request, as do 22 of the 35 against the base guard (the other 13 use the middle position). % src: r2_adaptive_{base,t_seed0}_search_null.json(.jsonl): T 26/26 position=prepend; base 22 prepend + 13 middle (filler before and after)
Unlike the one fixed filler of \cref{tab:ablation} ($0\%$), the control picks the best of up to 300 benign candidates per item by the guard's score, and that choice does most of the work. % src: src/fix/adaptive.py search_attack budget 300 (also for --null-search); confirm_log.txt [ablation] benign_tail 0/75
Each item's first candidate, drawn before any score is seen, clears the base guard on $28\%$\ci{17\%}{42\%} of items and T on $6\%$\ci{2\%}{16\%}, so benign text alone dilutes the request only a little. % src: paper/data/search_no_feedback.md (queries == 1: base_search_null 14/50, t_seed0_search_null 3/50; Wilson)

GCG clears T on all $20$ items it was run on, which raises the per-item worst case over all attacks against T to $49/50$ ($98\%$\ci{89\%}{100\%}). % src: r2_adaptive_t_seed0_gcg.json
We did not run GCG on B$+$T, so its worst case, $34/50$, is a lower bound.
