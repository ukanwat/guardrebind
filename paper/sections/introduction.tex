\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Safety guards for large language model (LLM) applications are small language models trained to answer one question about one piece of text: does this content violate a policy?
They sit in front of chat assistants, on retrieval pipelines and around agent tool calls, and their verdict is used as a control: cleared content is passed on, and in an agent loop an action may run without further review.
Recent guards make the policy itself an input.
Shieldstral~\citep{calvi2026shieldstral,mistral2026shieldstralblog} is a query--document guard: the developer supplies an \emph{instruction} (how strict to be) and a \emph{query} (a yes/no grading question such as ``does this content assist harmful activity?''), the untrusted content is inserted as the \emph{document}, and the guard answers whether the document is unsafe with respect to the query (\cref{fig:anatomy}); gpt-oss-safeguard~\citep{openai2025gptosssafeguard} reasons over a developer-written policy in the system role; Llama Guard~\citep{inan2023llama,meta2025llamaguard4} grades a conversation against a fixed taxonomy inside its own delimiters.
In all three, the content under review reaches the model through the same token stream as the structure that tells the model what to do with it.
The original Llama Guard paper already warns that the model ``may be susceptible to prompt injection attacks''~\citep{inan2023llama}, and the gpt-oss-safeguard report rates the 20B model's prompt-injection robustness at 0.512~\citep{openai2025gptosssafeguard}; neither says what such an attack looks like against a guard, or how to build a guard that resists it.

The \emph{labels} that delimit the query and the document (\texttt{<Query>:}, \texttt{<Document>:}) are, in the formats we study, plain text that the developer interpolates around untrusted content.
Nothing stops the content from containing the same labels.
When it does, the guard sees two complete grading tasks and grades the document in the last one (\cref{fig:anatomy}).
The harmful request is not removed, rewritten or hidden: it stays in the message that the guard approves and the downstream model receives.
We call this \emph{target rebinding}: the content changes which document the guard is grading, not the guard's judgement of that document.
We call the attacker's forged query and document a \emph{forged pair}, and the text of its document, which the guard grades in place of the content, the \emph{decoy}.

Forging a completed task followed by a new one is a known prompt-injection pattern against LLM-integrated applications~\citep{liu2024formalizing}, and forged template, conversational or judge-output structure is documented against agents, chat models and LLM judges~\citep{deng2026automating,chang2025chatinject,chen2024goal,li2025separator,shi2024optimizationbased,li2025llms,gros2026evaluating,tamber2025illusions,mccauley2025hat}; concurrent work forges verdict and authority fields in a judge's input~\citep{li2026same}.
An informal version of the core attack appeared in an earlier blog post~\citep{kanwat2026blog}.
We do not claim a new class of injection (\cref{sec:related} positions this work).
What has been missing is a guard-specific measurement: whether the failure is rebinding or persuasion, how widely it occurs across guard designs, and whether a guard can be trained to resist it.

\paragraph{Evidence.}
In a pre-registered study on Shieldstral, the forged pair clears 75 of 75 harmful AdvBench requests that the guard flags with no attack, drawn from rows held out of all exploratory runs ($100\%$\ci{95\%}{100\%}); a forged document alone clears $9\%$ and an instruction alone $0\%$ (\cref{sec:attack}). % src: results/confirm_log.txt
Across 40 decoys, bypass (the share of flagged requests that the attack gets cleared) falls as the decoy's own score rises (Spearman $\rho=-0.84$, decoy-bootstrap 95\% interval $[-0.93, -0.67]$), the dose--response that rebinding predicts (\cref{sec:binding}). % src: results/mech/dose_response_test_n150_d40_rho_ci.json
The construction transfers to gpt-oss-safeguard-20B and Llama Guard~4 (30/30 and 29/29 flagged AdvBench requests), and in our benchmark, \guardrebind{}, the strongest attack family clears $93$--$100\%$ of flagged items on each of five guards (\cref{sec:benchmark}). % src: results/confirm_crossmodel.csv; results/bench/v2/bypass.csv; paper/data/gpt_oss_strict.md
Segment-max self-distillation, which trains the guard to score content at least as high as the base guard scores its most harmful part, is trained on one half of the decoy and forged-query pools and tested on the other half.
On Shieldstral it changes bypass on appended attack families and decoys held out of training from $75.0\%$ to $0.8$--$1.6\%$ (two seeds). Detection and over-refusal point estimates stay within the pre-registered margins, but the intervals are too wide to confirm non-inferiority, and flags on benign content carrying a forged pair rise from $0.2\%$ to $0.7$--$0.8\%$, a small but statistically detectable cost. An attacker who enumerates all 654 attack forms, in any position, clears it on $8\%$ of items (\cref{sec:evaluation}). % src: r2_test_{base,t_seed0,t_seed1,bt_seed0}.json attacked_benign_fpr_benign_decoy, fixed decoy, train+heldout: 4, 16, 16, 19 of 2400; Fisher exact vs base p = 0.012 (T), 0.003 (B+T)
An attacker who can query the defended guard's score still breaks it: a score-guided search clears the trained guard on $94\%$ of items and, when the content is also wrapped in two learned \emph{boundary tokens} that content cannot produce, on $68\%$; with the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack~\citep{zou2023universal} the per-item worst case is $98\%$ (\cref{sec:adaptive,app:gcg}).
More than half of these wins need no forged structure: benign user prompts placed before the request, with no labels, clear the trained guard on $52\%$ of items and the base guard on $70\%$.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item[\textbf{C1}] \textbf{Target rebinding is structural.} On Shieldstral, only a complete forged query--document pair clears the guard; the bypass tracks the harm of the decoy; and it is not explained by recency, a single label syntax or the policy wording (\cref{sec:attack}).
  \item[\textbf{C2}] \textbf{It is widespread.} The attack transfers across three guard input designs, and \guardrebind{}, a grammar of 52 attack families and 5 controls, measures it across five guards with clean (no-attack) miss and false-positive rates alongside; every guard has a family above $93\%$ (\cref{sec:benchmark}).
  \item[\textbf{C3}] \textbf{Training generalises to held-out attacks but not to an adaptive attacker.} Segment-max self-distillation (T), with or without trained boundary tokens, closes the structural channel on appended attack families, decoys and forged queries held out of training, where a fail-closed structure detector and inference-time segment-max (taking the max over the content's parts at run time) both let $23\%$ of these attacks through and the detector also blocks about half of benign structured content. It does not hold against a score-guided search or GCG, and a plain-text control shows that more than half of the search's wins need no forged structure (\cref{sec:defense,sec:evaluation}).
\end{itemize}
